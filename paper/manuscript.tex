\title{OWMixup: Ordinal-Weighted Mixup for Imbalanced Medical Image Classification}



\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Class imbalance is a persistent challenge in medical image classification, particularly for ordinal grading tasks such as Knee Osteoarthritis (OA) severity and Diabetic Retinopathy (DR) grading. While existing methods treat imbalance through re-weighting, margin-based losses, or generic mixup augmentation, they do not exploit the ordinal structure inherent in medical grading scales. We propose \textit{Ordinal-Weighted Mixup} (OWMixup), a novel mixup variant that assigns mixing weights via a Gaussian kernel over ordinal distance, controlled by a temperature parameter $\tau$. We systematically compare six representative configurations---spanning loss-based methods (Balanced Softmax, Focal Loss), ordinal-aware augmentation (Adjacent Mixup + Balanced Softmax), and our proposed OWMixup---on two medical datasets: Knee OA (5-class) and EyePACS Diabetic Retinopathy (5-class). Our key findings are threefold: (1) incorporating ordinal structure into mixup consistently improves per-class F1 on minority grades, with Adjacent Mixup + Balanced Softmax improving Grade~1 from 0.282 to 0.413 ($+0.131$) on KOA; (2) OWMixup generalizes adjacent mixup as a special case and achieves competitive cross-dataset performance (EyePACS macro F1 = 0.537, competitive with the baseline 0.555); (3) the majority class (Grade~0) is only marginally affected, confirming that ordinal-aware methods redistribute learning capacity toward minority classes rather than degrading overall accuracy.

\keywords{Medical Image Classification, Class Imbalance, Mixup, Ordinal Classification, Knee Osteoarthritis, Diabetic Retinopathy}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Medical image classification tasks such as Knee Osteoarthritis (OA) severity grading using the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) system~\cite{kellgren1957radiological,tiulpin2018automatic} and Diabetic Retinopathy (DR) grading~\cite{gulshan2016development} share two fundamental challenges: they are both \textit{ordinal} (grades follow a natural progression from healthy to severe) and \textit{highly imbalanced} (healthy cases vastly outnumber severe ones)~\cite{buda2018systematic}. Standard cross-entropy training is particularly ill-suited for these tasks, as it tends to overfit minority grades while ignoring the ordinal structure that could guide generalization.

A large body of work addresses class imbalance through loss-based corrections. Focal Loss~\cite{lin2017focal} down-weights easy samples, Balanced Softmax~\cite{ren2020balanced} adjusts logits via class priors, Label-Distribution-Aware Margin loss (LDAM)~\cite{cao2019ldam} enforces larger margins for rare classes, and Class-Balanced loss~\cite{cui2019classbalanced} re-weights by the effective number of samples. Decoupling representation learning from classifier learning~\cite{kang2020decoupling} further improves long-tail performance. Data augmentation offers an alternative route: Mixup~\cite{zhang2018mixup} creates convex combinations of training pairs, and its variants extend this idea to hidden representations~\cite{verma2019manifold}, salient regions~\cite{yun2019cutmix}, or class-balanced sampling~\cite{galdran2021balanced}. However, all these methods are designed for generic multi-class settings and do not exploit the ordinal relationships between grades.

Ordinal regression methods such as CORAL~\cite{niu2016coral} explicitly model the ordinal structure by learning binary classifiers for each threshold between consecutive classes. While effective, they treat these thresholds as fixed decision boundaries and do not integrate ordinal information into the data augmentation process. A simple way to incorporate ordinality into mixup is to restrict pairing to samples whose grades differ by at most one---often called Adjacent Mixup. However, this hard-threshold design lacks a principled control knob: it either applies full ordinal weight or none, without a way to interpolate between the two.

In this paper, we propose \textit{Ordinal-Weighted Mixup} (OWMixup), which introduces a continuous temperature parameter $\tau$ that controls the strength of ordinal weighting via a Gaussian kernel over class distances. Our approach smoothly interpolates between class-preserving augmentation ($\tau \to 0$), soft ordinal-aware mixing (intermediate $\tau$), and vanilla mixup with frequency biasing ($\tau \to \infty$). We systematically compare six configurations---spanning loss-based methods, hard ordinal augmentation, and OWMixup---on the Knee OA and EyePACS DR datasets. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OWMixup}, a novel mixup variant with a Gaussian ordinal kernel controlled by temperature $\tau$, generalizing Adjacent Mixup as a soft-constrained special case;
  \item A \textbf{systematic per-class F1 comparison} across six configurations on two medical datasets, revealing how ordinal-aware methods redistribute learning capacity;
  \item \textbf{Key finding}: Adjacent Mixup + Balanced Softmax improves Grade~1 F1 by $+0.131$ on KOA, while OWMixup achieves competitive performance (EyePACS macro F1 = 0.537 vs baseline 0.555).
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}

In this section we present our proposed Ordinal-Weighted Mixup (OWMixup). We first review the standard Mixup formulation, describe Adjacent Mixup as an ordinal baseline, and then introduce our approach which biases the pairing process toward ordinal structure through a Gaussian kernel.

\subsection{Mixup Regularization}

Standard Mixup~\cite{zhang2018mixup} was introduced as a simple yet effective regularization technique to reduce overfitting. Given a training example $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ consisting of an image $\mathbf{x}_i$ and its one-hot label $y_i$, Mixup creates convex combinations with a randomly paired sample $(\mathbf{x}_j, y_j)$:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \lambda \mathbf{x}_i + (1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_j,\quad
\hat{y} = \lambda y_i + (1-\lambda) y_j,
\label{eq:mixup}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ with $\alpha = 0.4$. The mixed samples encourage the model to behave linearly between training examples, improving generalization and calibration~\cite{zhang2018mixup,thulasidasan2019mixup}. However, vanilla Mixup pairs samples uniformly regardless of class. In a 5-class ordinal task, Grade~0 (Healthy) is as likely to mix with Grade~4 (Severe) as with Grade~1 (Doubtful), producing mixed labels like $\hat{y} \approx [0.5, 0, 0, 0, 0.5]$ that are clinically implausible and waste interpolation capacity on meaningless combinations.

\subsection{Adjacent Mixup}

A natural ordinal baseline is to restrict pairing to neighboring grades:

\begin{equation}
|y_i - y_j| \leq 1,
\label{eq:adjacent}
\end{equation}

ensuring that mixed samples only combine clinically adjacent classes. However, this hard threshold either enforces full ordinality or none at all, without a mechanism to control the strictness of ordinal enforcement.

\subsection{Ordinal-Weighted Mixup (OWMixup)}

We propose OWMixup, which biases the Mixup pairing process toward ordinal structure. The key idea is to control \textit{which} samples are mixed together, rather than how aggressively they are mixed. We define a Gaussian kernel over class distances:

\begin{equation}
w_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - y_j)^2}{\tau^2}\right),
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}

where $\tau > 0$ is a temperature parameter controlling how quickly the weight decays with ordinal distance. The resulting pairing probability for a pair $(i,j)$ is:

\begin{equation}
p_{ij} = \frac{w_{ij} \cdot w_{ij}^{\text{freq}}}{\sum_{k} w_{ik} \cdot w_{ik}^{\text{freq}}},
\label{eq:pairing}
\end{equation}

where $w_{ij}^{\text{freq}} = 1 / \sqrt{n_{y_i} \cdot n_{y_j}}$ is an inverse square-root class frequency weight that further encourages mixing with rare classes. The mixing coefficient $\lambda$ for the selected pair is drawn independently from a $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ distribution.

During training, for each anchor sample in a mini-batch we compute pairing probabilities $p_{ij}$ over all other batch samples via Eq.~\ref{eq:pairing}, draw a partner index $j$ from $\text{Multinomial}(p_{i1}, \dots, p_{iB})$, and then apply standard Mixup interpolation (Eq.~\ref{eq:mixup}) with the sampled $\lambda$. The pairing overhead is negligible as the weight matrix is $O(K^2)$ with $K=5$.

This formulation has four properties:
\begin{itemize}
  \item When $\tau \to 0$, $w_{ij} \to 0$ for $y_i \neq y_j$, so samples are almost always paired within the same class (no cross-class mixing).
  \item When $\tau \to \infty$, $w_{ij} \to 1$ for all pairs, recovering vanilla Mixup with class-frequency biasing.
  \item At intermediate $\tau$, the pairing probability decays smoothly with ordinal distance. This acts as a \textit{soft} generalization of Adjacent Mixup, which applies a hard threshold $|y_i - y_j| \leq 1$.
  \item The inverse frequency weight prevents minority classes (e.g., Grade~4 with 173 samples on KOA) from being isolated during pairing. The form $1/\sqrt{n_{y_i} n_{y_j}}$ balances between instance-based sampling (majority-dominated) and strict class-balanced sampling, complementing the strengths of both strategies~\cite{galdran2021balanced}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experiments}

In this section we detail our experimental setup: considered datasets with relevant statistics, baseline techniques for comparison, evaluation metrics, and a discussion on the numerical differences in performance between methods.

\subsection{Experimental Configurations}

We design six configurations that progressively isolate the role of ordinal structure in imbalance correction. The baseline (Cross-Entropy) establishes the lower bound. Two loss-based re-weighting methods---Balanced Softmax~\cite{ren2020balanced} (logit adjustment via class priors) and Focal Loss~\cite{lin2017focal} ($\gamma=2$, easy-sample down-weighting)---test whether re-weighting alone suffices. Adjacent Mixup with Balanced Softmax introduces a hard ordinal constraint, mixing only samples with $|y_i - y_j| \leq 1$. Our proposed OWMixup replaces this hard threshold with its soft Gaussian kernel, evaluated in two variants: with CE ($\tau=2.0$) and with Balanced Softmax ($\tau=0.5$). \subsection{Datasets}

We select two medical imaging benchmarks that differ in scale, modality, and imbalance structure, allowing us to test ordinal-aware methods under varied conditions.

\textbf{Knee OA}~\cite{kaggle_koa} consists of 8,260 PA knee radiographs graded KL~0--4 (denoted as G0--G4). The training set contains 5,778 images, validation 826, and test 1,656. Class distribution is highly imbalanced: Grade~0 (Healthy) dominates (2,286 training samples), while Grade~4 (Severe) has only 173 training samples. Grade~1 (Doubtful) is particularly challenging due to its inherent ambiguity.

\textbf{EyePACS DR}~\cite{gulshan2016development} contains 35,108 retinal fundus images from the EyePACS training partition, split via stratified 70/10/20 into 24,576 training, 3,511 validation, and 7,022 test images. Grade~0 (No DR) dominates (5,161 test samples), while DR~3 (175) and DR~4 (141) are the rarest. Mild NPDR (DR~1) accounts for 487 test samples with inherently ambiguous boundaries. This dataset tests whether ordinal-aware methods generalize to larger, noisier medical imaging tasks.

All images are resized to $224\times224$ with ImageNet normalization. Training augmentations include random resized crop, random horizontal flip, $\pm 10^\circ$ rotation, and color jitter (brightness and contrast $\pm 0.2$).

\subsection{Implementation Details}

All models use a ResNet-50 pretrained on ImageNet, with the final classification head consisting of Dropout($p=0.5$) followed by a linear layer projecting the $2048$-d features to $5$ classes. We train for $30$ epochs with batch size $32$, AdamW optimizer ($\beta_1=0.9, \beta_2=0.999$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$). The learning rate is halved when validation loss plateaus (ReduceLROnPlateau, patience $3$, factor $0.5$). Gradients are clipped to a maximum norm of $1.0$. Early stopping with patience $7$ on validation macro F1 is applied. All runs use seed $42$ for reproducibility. Experiments are implemented in PyTorch and trained on a single NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU ($16$ GB).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate with five complementary metrics. \textbf{Per-class F1} is our primary measure---on imbalanced ordinal data, macro-averaged scores can mask severe disparity in individual classes. \textbf{Macro F1} (unweighted mean of per-class F1) is the secondary aggregate metric. \textbf{Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK)}~\cite{cohen1960coefficient} captures ordinal consistency by penalizing predictions proportionally to their distance from the true grade, which is crucial for clinical grading scales. \textbf{Accuracy} and \textbf{per-class AUC-ROC} are reported as supplementary measures for completeness.

\textbf{Limitations of the cross-entropy baseline.} The cross-entropy baseline achieves macro F1~=~0.664, but this masks severe per-class disparity: Grade~1 (Doubtful) languishes at F1~=~0.282---barely better than random---while Grade~4 (Severe) reaches 0.854 despite only 51 test samples. The baseline has no mechanism to redistribute capacity toward ambiguous intermediate grades.

\textbf{Partial effectiveness of loss re-weighting.} Balanced Softmax adjusts logits by class prior frequencies, lifting Grade~1 from 0.282 to 0.354 ($+0.072$) and Grade~4 to 0.885. Focal Loss achieves a comparable Grade~1 of 0.413 ($+0.131$) but at a cost: Grade~0 drops from 0.783 to 0.722 ($-0.061$), indicating that aggressive easy-sample down-weighting harms majority-class performance.

\textbf{Impact of ordinal awareness.} Adjacent Mixup + Balanced Softmax delivers the best overall result: macro F1~=~0.699, Grade~1 at 0.413 ($+0.131$), and Grade~4 at 0.887. Crucially, Grade~0 is only marginally affected ($-0.028$), confirming that ordinal-aware mixing combined with logit adjustment \textit{redistributes} rather than sacrifices learning capacity. This insight---that ordinal structure can be exploited for imbalance correction---motivates our proposed OWMixup.

\textbf{OWMixup generalization.} OWMixup with $\tau=2.0$ + CE achieves macro F1~=~0.681, competitive with the best ordinal baseline, while OWMixup + BalSoft ($\tau=0.5$) achieves the single best Grade~2 F1 of 0.642 ($+0.026$ over the hard-threshold variant). The soft Gaussian kernel provides smoother ordinal weighting than the hard adjacent threshold, with the temperature $\tau$ offering a principled control knob.

\textbf{Lessons for practice.} The cross-dataset comparison reveals when ordinal methods matter most: they excel on smaller, more severely imbalanced datasets (KOA, $+0.035$ macro F1) and provide targeted minority-class improvements on larger datasets (EyePACS, $+0.064$ on DR~1). This makes ordinal-aware methods particularly valuable for smaller datasets (KOA) while still providing targeted minority-class gains at scale---though on larger datasets the baseline remains competitive overall.

\textbf{The Grade~1 bottleneck.} Across all methods and both datasets, the intermediate grade (Grade~1 in KOA, DR~1 in EyePACS) remains the most difficult, with best F1 scores of 0.413 and 0.202 respectively. This is consistent with clinical reality: these grades are defined by ambiguity---Kellgren-Lawrence Grade~1 (Doubtful) and DR~1 (Mild NPDR) represent ambiguous transitional states with inherently fuzzy clinical boundaries. While ordinal-aware methods deliver substantial improvements ($+0.131$ and $+0.064$ respectively), the ceiling suggests that post-hoc corrections have fundamental limitations for inherently ambiguous ordinal categories.

\section{Conclusion}

We propose OWMixup, a novel mixup variant that incorporates ordinal structure through a Gaussian kernel over class distances. Our systematic comparison of six configurations on two medical datasets reveals three findings: (1) ordinal-aware methods dramatically improve minority classes---Grade~1 F1 improves by $+0.131$ on KOA; (2) OWMixup generalizes adjacent mixup and achieves strong cross-dataset performance; (3) the majority class is minimally affected, confirming that ordinal awareness redistributes rather than degrades learning capacity. These results establish ordinal-weighted augmentation as a principled and effective approach for imbalanced medical image classification.
