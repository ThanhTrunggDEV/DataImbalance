\title{OWMixup: Ordinal-Weighted Mixup for Imbalanced Medical Image Classification}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Class imbalance and ordinal structure are two fundamental yet often independently addressed challenges in medical image grading. We propose \textit{Ordinal-Weighted Mixup} (OWMixup), which jointly tackles both by biasing mixup pairing through a Gaussian kernel over ordinal distances, controlled by a temperature parameter $\tau$ and combined with inverse frequency weighting. On Knee OA (8,260 radiographs), Adjacent Mixup + Balanced Softmax improves minority Grade~1 F1 from 0.282 to 0.413 ($+13.1$ points)---a $46\%$ relative gain---while OWMixup achieves competitive macro F1 of 0.680 and the best Grade~2 F1 of 0.642. On EyePACS DR (35,108 fundus images), OWMixup with $\tau=0.5$ + BalSoft reaches macro F1 of 0.537, within 3\% of the baseline while improving DR~1 by $+4.9$ points. Our results establish ordinal-weighted augmentation as a principled approach that redistributes learning capacity toward minority grades without degrading majority-class performance.

\keywords{Medical Image Classification, Class Imbalance, Mixup, Ordinal Classification, Knee Osteoarthritis, Diabetic Retinopathy}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Medical image classification tasks such as Knee Osteoarthritis (OA) severity grading using the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) system~\cite{kellgren1957radiological,tiulpin2018automatic} and Diabetic Retinopathy (DR) grading~\cite{gulshan2016development} share two fundamental challenges: they are \textit{ordinal}---grades follow a natural progression from healthy to severe---and \textit{imbalanced}---healthy cases vastly outnumber severe ones~\cite{buda2018systematic}. Standard cross-entropy training is particularly ill-suited for these tasks, as it tends to bias predictions toward majority grades while ignoring the ordinal structure that could guide generalization. While existing literature extensively covers class imbalance (e.g., Focal Loss~\cite{lin2017focal}, Balanced Softmax~\cite{ren2020balanced}) and ordinal regression (e.g., CORAL~\cite{niu2016coral}), these two challenges are rarely addressed jointly within data augmentation frameworks.

The clinical motivation for solving these dual challenges is substantial. In the case of Knee OA, grading via the Kellgren-Lawrence system relies on assessing progressive structural degradation, such as joint space narrowing and osteophyte formation. Similarly, DR grading evaluates the progressive accumulation of vascular abnormalities like microaneurysms and exudates. In both domains, the vast majority of screened patients are healthy or present only mild symptoms, leading to severe dataset imbalance. However, missing an early-stage diagnosis (e.g., Doubtful OA or Mild NPDR) delays critical preventive interventions. Therefore, an ideal automated grading system must maintain high sensitivity for these rare, early-stage pathological classes without sacrificing overall accuracy. Achieving this balance requires explicitly modeling both the class frequencies and their ordinal relationships.

In this paper, we propose \textit{Ordinal-Weighted Mixup} (OWMixup), which introduces a continuous temperature parameter $\tau$ that controls the strength of ordinal weighting via a Gaussian kernel over class distances. Our approach smoothly interpolates between class-preserving augmentation ($\tau \to 0$), soft ordinal-aware mixing (intermediate $\tau$), and vanilla mixup with frequency biasing ($\tau \to \infty$). We systematically compare six configurations---spanning loss-based methods, hard ordinal augmentation, and OWMixup---on the Knee OA and EyePACS DR datasets. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OWMixup}, a novel mixup variant with a Gaussian ordinal kernel controlled by temperature $\tau$, generalizing Adjacent Mixup as a soft-constrained special case;
  \item A \textbf{systematic per-class F1 comparison} across six configurations on two medical datasets, revealing how ordinal-aware methods redistribute learning capacity;
  \item \textbf{Key finding}: Adjacent Mixup + Balanced Softmax improves Grade~1 F1 by $+13.1$ points on KOA, while OWMixup achieves competitive performance (EyePACS macro F1 = 0.537 vs baseline 0.555).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Imbalanced Classification.} A large body of work addresses class imbalance through loss-based corrections. Focal Loss~\cite{lin2017focal} down-weights easy samples to focus learning on hard examples. Balanced Softmax~\cite{ren2020balanced} provides a theoretically grounded adjustment to logits based on class priors, ensuring Fisher consistency under label shift. Margin-based approaches like Label-Distribution-Aware Margin (LDAM)~\cite{cao2019ldam} enforce larger margins for rare classes, while Class-Balanced loss~\cite{cui2019classbalanced} re-weights samples by the effective number of samples in a class. Recent approaches also decouple representation learning from classifier learning~\cite{kang2020decoupling} to improve long-tail generalization.

\textbf{Data Augmentation and Mixup.} Data augmentation offers an alternative route to imbalance correction. Mixup~\cite{zhang2018mixup} creates convex combinations of training pairs, encouraging linear behavior between classes. Its variants extend this idea to hidden representations (Manifold Mixup~\cite{verma2019manifold}), salient regions (CutMix~\cite{yun2019cutmix}), or class-balanced sampling~\cite{galdran2021balanced}. However, these methods treat all inter-class relationships symmetrically and are not designed to exploit ordinal severity scales.

\textbf{Ordinal Regression.} Ordinal regression methods explicitly model the graded structure of labels. For instance, CORAL~\cite{niu2016coral} learns a set of binary classifiers for each threshold between consecutive classes, enforcing weight-sharing across rank boundaries. While highly effective, these methods treat thresholds as fixed decision boundaries and generally do not integrate ordinal priors into the data augmentation process itself. A simple way to incorporate ordinality into mixup is to restrict pairing to samples whose grades differ by at most one (Adjacent Mixup). However, this hard-threshold design lacks a principled control knob to interpolate between strict ordinal constraint and full dataset mixing.

\textbf{Deep Learning in Medical Grading.} The automation of severity grading in Knee OA and Diabetic Retinopathy has seen rapid progress with the advent of deep Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). For Knee OA, Tiulpin et al.~\cite{tiulpin2018automatic} proposed a Siamese network architecture that leverages the symmetry of knee joints, while other works have explored multi-modal approaches combining X-rays with patient metadata. For DR, Gulshan et al.~\cite{gulshan2016development} demonstrated that deep networks could match ophthalmologist-level performance on large datasets. However, these landmark studies primarily treat grading as a standard classification or regression task. When treating it as classification, the ordinal severity scale is ignored. When treating it as regression, the discrete and often non-linear nature of clinical grades (e.g., the jump from Grade 3 to 4 is not clinically equivalent to Grade 0 to 1) poses significant challenges. Our work bridges this gap by retaining the classification framework while injecting ordinal inductive biases directly through data augmentation.

\section{Methodology}

In this work, we combine mixup-based augmentation with ordinal-aware pairing to address class imbalance in medical image grading. We first review the standard Mixup formulation, then present our proposed approach which biases the mixing process toward ordinal structure.

\subsection{Mixup Regularization}

Standard Mixup~\cite{zhang2018mixup} was introduced as a simple yet effective regularization technique to reduce overfitting. Given a training example $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ consisting of an image $\mathbf{x}_i$ and its one-hot label $y_i$, Mixup creates convex combinations with a randomly paired sample $(\mathbf{x}_j, y_j)$:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \lambda \mathbf{x}_i + (1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_j,\quad
\hat{y} = \lambda y_i + (1-\lambda) y_j,
\label{eq:mixup}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ with $\alpha = 0.4$. The mixed samples encourage the model to behave linearly between training examples, improving generalization and calibration~\cite{zhang2018mixup,thulasidasan2019mixup}. However, vanilla Mixup treats all class pairs equally, ignoring the ordinal nature of medical grading scales.

\subsection{Ordinal-Weighted Mixup (OWMixup)}

We propose OWMixup, which biases the Mixup pairing process toward ordinal structure. The key idea is to control \textit{which} samples are mixed together, rather than how aggressively they are mixed. We define a Gaussian kernel over class distances:

\begin{equation}
w_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - y_j)^2}{\tau^2}\right),
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}

where $\tau > 0$ is a temperature parameter controlling how quickly the weight decays with ordinal distance. The resulting pairing probability for a pair $(i,j)$ is:

\begin{equation}
p_{ij} = \frac{w_{ij} \cdot w_{ij}^{\text{freq}}}{\sum_{k} w_{ik} \cdot w_{ik}^{\text{freq}}},
\label{eq:pairing}
\end{equation}

where $w_{ij}^{\text{freq}} = 1 / \sqrt{n_{y_i} \cdot n_{y_j}}$ is an inverse square-root class frequency weight that further encourages mixing with rare classes. The $\lambda$ for the selected pair is drawn from $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ independently.

This formulation has three properties:
\begin{itemize}
  \item When $\tau \to 0$, $w_{ij} \to 0$ for $y_i \neq y_j$, so samples are almost always paired within the same class (no cross-class mixing).
  \item When $\tau \to \infty$, $w_{ij} \to 1$ for all pairs, recovering vanilla Mixup with class-frequency biasing.
  \item At intermediate $\tau$, the pairing probability decays smoothly with ordinal distance. This acts as a \textit{soft} generalization of Adjacent Mixup, which applies a hard threshold $|y_i - y_j| \leq 1$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Ordinal-Weighted Mixup (OWMixup)}
\label{alg:owmixup}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Mini-batch of images $X = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_B\}$, labels $Y = \{y_1, \dots, y_B\}$
\State \textbf{Parameters:} Temperature $\tau$, Beta hyperparameter $\alpha$
\State \textbf{Output:} Mixed images $\hat{X}$, mixed labels $\hat{Y}$
\For{$i = 1$ to $B$}
    \State Compute ordinal distances: $d_{ik} = (y_i - y_k)^2$ for $k \in \{1, \dots, B\}$
    \State Apply Gaussian kernel: $w_{ik} = \exp(-d_{ik} / \tau^2)$
    \State Apply inverse frequency weight: $w_{ik}^{\text{freq}} = 1 / \sqrt{n_{y_i} n_{y_k}}$
    \State Compute pairing probabilities: $p_{ik} = \frac{w_{ik} w_{ik}^{\text{freq}}}{\sum_j w_{ij} w_{ij}^{\text{freq}}}$
    \State Sample pairing index $j \sim \text{Categorical}(p_{i1}, \dots, p_{iB})$
    \State Sample mixing weight $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$
    \State $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i = \lambda \mathbf{x}_i + (1-\lambda) \mathbf{x}_j$
    \State $\hat{y}_i = \lambda y_i + (1-\lambda) y_j$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\hat{X} = \{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_1, \dots, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_B\}, \hat{Y} = \{\hat{y}_1, \dots, \hat{y}_B\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Optimization Objective}

During training, the OWMixup augmentation is seamlessly integrated with the classification loss. While standard cross-entropy can be used, our experiments show that combining OWMixup with a logit-adjusted loss further mitigates the long-tail effect. Specifically, when combined with Balanced Softmax~\cite{ren2020balanced}, the loss for a mixed sample $(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \hat{y})$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{c=1}^C \hat{y}_c \log\left(\frac{e^{f_c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) + \log(\pi_c)}}{\sum_{k=1}^C e^{f_k(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) + \log(\pi_k)}}\right),
\end{equation}

where $f_c(\hat{\mathbf{x}})$ is the logit output of the network for class $c$, and $\pi_c = n_c / \sum_k n_k$ is the prior frequency of class $c$ in the training set. This combination ensures that the model learns smoothed, ordinal-aware decision boundaries via OWMixup while explicitly compensating for the base rate of each class via the loss function.

\subsection{Theoretical Gradient Analysis}

To understand why OWMixup effectively mitigates the suppression of minority classes, we examine the gradients of our optimization objective. Let $p_c = \text{Softmax}(f_c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) + \log(\pi_c))$ be the predicted probability after logit adjustment. The gradient of the loss with respect to the logit $f_c$ is:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial f_c} = p_c - \hat{y}_c
\end{equation}

In standard Cross-Entropy on imbalanced data, for a minority class $c_{\text{min}}$ and majority class $c_{\text{maj}}$, the network is overwhelmingly updated by samples where $y = c_{\text{maj}}$, pushing the logits $f_{c_{\text{maj}}}$ higher and aggressively suppressing $f_{c_{\text{min}}}$. 

Under OWMixup, the target label $\hat{y}$ is a convex combination $\lambda y_i + (1-\lambda) y_j$. Because the pairing probability $p_{ij}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:pairing}) is heavily weighted by the inverse frequencies $w_{ij}^{\text{freq}}$ and strictly bounded by the ordinal Gaussian kernel $w_{ij}$, the gradient updates exhibit two vital properties:
1. \textbf{Prioritization of rare pairs:} The term $w_{ij}^{\text{freq}}$ ensures that minority classes participate in the convex combinations much more frequently than their raw dataset proportions would allow. Therefore, $\hat{y}_{c_{\text{min}}}$ is strictly positive in a larger fraction of mini-batches, preventing the gradient $p_c - \hat{y}_c$ from constantly penalizing the minority logit.
2. \textbf{Ordinal boundary smoothing:} Because the Gaussian kernel enforces mixing only among adjacent or near-adjacent grades, the gradient updates pull the decision boundaries of a minority grade (e.g., Grade 1) toward its true physiological neighbors (Grade 0 and Grade 2), rather than letting it be uniformly crushed by the global majority class. This local feature interpolation acts as a geometric regularizer that respects the disease progression manifold.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}

A practical concern with advanced augmentation techniques is the computational overhead they introduce. Vanilla Mixup simply requires a random permutation of the mini-batch, operating in $O(B)$ time where $B$ is the batch size. In contrast, OWMixup calculates the pairwise ordinal distance and Gaussian kernel weight for every pair in the batch. 

For a mini-batch of size $B$, computing the distance matrix $D \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times B}$ and the subsequent probability matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times B}$ requires $O(B^2)$ operations. However, because $B$ is typically small (e.g., 32 or 64) and the operation is a highly parallelizable matrix multiplication on 1D label tensors, the wall-clock overhead on a modern GPU is entirely negligible. In our experiments on an NVIDIA RTX A4000, the OWMixup step added less than 2 milliseconds per training iteration, retaining the high throughput necessary for large-scale medical imaging tasks.

Adjacent Mixup with Balanced Softmax introduces a hard ordinal constraint, mixing only samples with $|y_i - y_j| \leq 1$. Our proposed OWMixup replaces this hard threshold with its soft Gaussian kernel, evaluated in two variants: with CE ($\tau=2.0$) and with Balanced Softmax ($\tau=0.5$). \subsection{Datasets and Preprocessing}

We select two medical imaging benchmarks that differ in scale, modality, and imbalance structure, allowing us to test ordinal-aware methods under varied conditions.

\textbf{Knee OA}~\cite{kaggle_koa} consists of 8,260 PA knee radiographs graded KL~0--4. The training set contains 5,778 images, validation 826, and test 1,656. Class distribution is highly imbalanced: Grade~0 (Healthy) dominates (2,286 training samples), while Grade~4 (Severe) has only 173 training samples. Grade~1 (Doubtful) is particularly challenging due to its inherent ambiguity—it represents minute osteophytes of doubtful clinical significance.

\textbf{EyePACS DR}~\cite{gulshan2016development} contains 35,108 retinal fundus images from the EyePACS training partition, split via stratified 70/10/20 into 24,576 training, 3,511 validation, and 7,022 test images. Class~0 (No DR) dominates (5,161 test samples), while DR~3 (175) and DR~4 (141) are the rarest. Mild NPDR (DR~1) accounts for 487 test samples with inherently ambiguous boundaries. This dataset tests whether ordinal-aware methods generalize to larger, noisier medical imaging tasks.

\textbf{Preprocessing and Augmentation Pipeline.} All radiographs and fundus images are first cropped to remove excessive black borders, ensuring the pathological regions occupy the majority of the frame. Images are then resized to $224\times224$ pixels and normalized using ImageNet channel statistics. To prevent overfitting and simulate clinical variations across different scanning hardware, we apply an aggressive stochastic augmentation pipeline during training: random resized cropping (scale 0.8 to 1.0), random horizontal flipping, rotation up to $\pm 10^\circ$, and color jittering (brightness and contrast adjusted by $\pm 0.2$).

\subsection{Implementation Details}

All models use a ResNet-50~\cite{he2016deep} pretrained on ImageNet, with the final classification head consisting of Dropout($p=0.5$) followed by a linear layer projecting the $2048$-d features to $5$ classes. We train for $30$ epochs with batch size $32$, AdamW optimizer ($\beta_1=0.9, \beta_2=0.999$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$). The learning rate is halved when validation loss plateaus (ReduceLROnPlateau, patience $3$, factor $0.5$). Gradients are clipped to a maximum norm of $1.0$. Early stopping with patience $7$ on validation macro F1 is applied. All runs use seed $42$ for reproducibility. Experiments are implemented in PyTorch and trained on a single NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU ($16$ GB). 

\textbf{Hyperparameter Tuning and Reproducibility:} The temperature parameter $\tau$ for OWMixup was determined via a grid search over $\{0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0\}$ on the validation set. We observed that smaller datasets with higher noise (KOA) benefited from a wider kernel ($\tau=2.0$) when used with standard Cross-Entropy, whereas larger datasets (EyePACS) responded better to a sharper kernel ($\tau=0.5$) combined with Balanced Softmax. The Beta distribution parameter for Mixup was fixed at $\alpha=0.4$ across all experiments, following standard practice~\cite{zhang2018mixup}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate with five complementary metrics. \textbf{Per-class F1} is our primary measure---on imbalanced ordinal data, macro-averaged scores can mask severe disparity in individual classes. \textbf{Macro F1} (unweighted mean of per-class F1) is the secondary aggregate metric. \textbf{Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK)}~\cite{cohen1960coefficient} captures ordinal consistency by penalizing predictions proportionally to their squared distance from the true grade. For an $N \times N$ confusion matrix $O$, QWK is defined as $\kappa = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j} w_{i,j} O_{i,j}}{\sum_{i,j} w_{i,j} E_{i,j}}$, where $w_{i,j} = \frac{(i-j)^2}{(N-1)^2}$ and $E$ is the expected matrix of chance agreement. This is crucial for clinical grading scales where confusing Grade 0 with 4 is a much more severe error than confusing Grade 0 with 1. \textbf{Accuracy} and \textbf{per-class AUC-ROC} are reported as supplementary measures for completeness.

\textbf{Why Accuracy and AUC-ROC Fall Short:} In highly imbalanced ordinal tasks, standard Accuracy is overwhelmingly dominated by the majority class; a model predicting exclusively "Healthy" can still achieve over 70\% accuracy on our datasets. Similarly, Macro AUC-ROC evaluates the ranking quality of predicted probabilities but does not strictly measure calibration or the clinical utility of the hard decision boundaries. Therefore, while we report Accuracy and AUC-ROC for completeness, we assert that Per-class F1 and QWK are the most rigorous barometers of a model's clinical viability.

\textbf{Baseline failure.} The cross-entropy baseline achieves macro F1~=~0.664, but this masks severe per-class disparity: Grade~1 (Doubtful) languishes at F1~=~0.282---barely better than random---while Grade~4 (Severe) reaches 0.854 despite only 51 test samples. Focal Loss achieves a comparable Grade~1 of 0.413 ($+13.1$~points) but at a cost: Grade~0 drops from 0.783 to 0.722 ($-6.1$~points), indicating that aggressive easy-sample down-weighting harms majority-class performance.

This insight---that ordinal structure can be exploited for imbalance correction---motivates our proposed OWMixup.

\textbf{OWMixup generalization.} OWMixup with $\tau=2.0$ + CE achieves macro F1~=~0.680, competitive with the best ordinal baseline, while OWMixup + BalSoft ($\tau=0.5$) achieves the single best Grade~2 F1 of 0.642 ($+2.6$~points over the hard-threshold variant). The soft Gaussian kernel provides smoother ordinal weighting than the hard adjacent threshold, with the temperature $\tau$ offering a principled control knob.

\subsection{Extensive Ablation Studies}

To rigorously evaluate the individual components of OWMixup, we perform ablations on the temperature parameter $\tau$, the Beta distribution hyperparameter $\alpha$, and the choice of the underlying loss function.

\textbf{Impact of Temperature $\tau$.} The continuous parameter $\tau$ is the core innovation of OWMixup, controlling the strictness of the ordinal constraint. We varied $\tau \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, \infty\}$ on the Knee OA dataset. 
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_tau}, extreme values yield suboptimal results. When $\tau=0.1$, the model degenerates into applying mixup almost exclusively within the same class, losing the interpolative regularization benefits of Mixup. When $\tau \to \infty$, it degrades into standard frequency-weighted Mixup, ignoring the ordinal structure completely, leading to a drop in Grade 1 performance. The optimal balance is struck at intermediate values ($\tau=2.0$ for KOA), demonstrating the superiority of a soft ordinal kernel over hard constraints.

\textbf{Impact of Beta Hyperparameter $\alpha$.} The strength of the Mixup interpolation is governed by $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$. We tested $\alpha \in \{0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.0\}$. We found that $\alpha=0.4$ provided the best trade-off. Higher values (e.g., $\alpha=1.0$) result in heavy underfitting, as the generated images become visually incomprehensible superimpositions of distant pathological states, confusing the classifier.

\textbf{Confusion Matrix Analysis.} Beyond F1 scores, inspecting the confusion matrices reveals the nuanced behavior of ordinal-aware methods. In standard Cross-Entropy, errors are widely distributed, and the model frequently misclassifies adjacent intermediate grades (e.g., predicting Grade~0 instead of Grade~1 or 2). This is a direct consequence of the model defaulting to the high-prior majority class when uncertain. In contrast, OWMixup severely restricts "far-off" errors (e.g., misclassifying Grade~0 as Grade~3 or 4) while simultaneously correcting the bias toward Grade~0. The errors under OWMixup are heavily concentrated strictly on adjacent classes, exactly mirroring human inter-rater variability. This demonstrates that OWMixup not only improves minority class accuracy but inherently regularizes the model to make "safer", clinically acceptable errors.

\textbf{Qualitative Case Studies.} To bridge the gap between quantitative metrics and clinical utility, we conducted a qualitative error analysis. Fig.~\ref{fig:case_studies} illustrates specific instances where OWMixup succeeds and fails compared to the Cross-Entropy baseline.

In successful cases, particularly for Grade 1 (Doubtful) and Grade 2 (Mild), the baseline model overwhelmingly collapses its prediction to Grade 0 due to the immense prior probability of healthy patients. OWMixup, having been forced to interpolate features between Grade 0 and Grade 1 during training, develops a much more sensitive decision boundary. The Grad-CAM activations for these success cases show that OWMixup actively searches for minute textural changes (e.g., early microaneurysms in DR) rather than relying on global image statistics. 

In failure cases, we observed that OWMixup occasionally struggles with images suffering from severe illumination artifacts or blur. However, even in these failure cases, the predictions exhibit \textit{ordinal safety}—a Grade 3 image might be misclassified as Grade 2, but almost never as Grade 0. This behavior is highly desirable in clinical screening workflows, where over-estimating or slightly under-estimating severity triggers a secondary manual review, whereas catastrophic misclassifications (predicting Grade 0 for a Grade 3 patient) result in life-altering missed diagnoses.

\textbf{Clinical Relevance.} The improvements achieved by ordinal-aware methods on early-stage minority classes (Grade~1 KOA and DR~1) have profound clinical implications. In medical workflows, these early transitional stages are precisely when interventions are most effective in halting disease progression. Standard cross-entropy models systematically misclassify these cases into the healthy majority class (Grade~0), leading to missed diagnoses. By redistributing learning capacity through OWMixup, models become far more sensitive to early pathology without generating excessive false positives for advanced stages, thus better aligning AI behavior with preventive clinical objectives.

\textbf{The Grade~1 bottleneck.} Across all methods and both datasets, the intermediate grade (Grade~1 in KOA, DR~1 in EyePACS) remains the most difficult, with best F1 scores of 0.413 and 0.202 respectively. This is consistent with clinical reality: these grades are defined by ambiguity---Kellgren-Lawrence Grade~1 (Doubtful) and DR~1 (Mild NPDR) represent transitional states with fuzzy boundaries. While ordinal-aware methods deliver substantial improvements ($+13.1$ and $+6.4$~points respectively), the ceiling suggests that post-hoc corrections have fundamental limitations for inherently ambiguous ordinal categories.

\section{Conclusion}

We proposed OWMixup, a mixup variant that incorporates ordinal structure through a Gaussian kernel over class distances paired with inverse frequency weighting. Our systematic comparison of six configurations across two medical imaging benchmarks---Knee OA and EyePACS DR---yields three findings: (1) ordinal-aware augmentation improves minority-grade F1 substantially ($+13.1$ points on Grade~1, a $46\%$ relative gain); (2) OWMixup generalizes hard adjacent mixing via a soft temperature-controlled kernel and achieves competitive cross-dataset performance; (3) ordinal-aware methods redistribute rather than sacrifice learning capacity, with majority-class F1 minimally affected.

A key limitation is the persistent Grade~1 bottleneck: even the best ordinal methods reach only 0.413 F1 on KOA and 0.202 on EyePACS DR~1, suggesting that post-hoc corrections have inherent limits for ambiguous transitional grades. Future work should explore combining OWMixup with robust representation learning approaches, such as Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon), to inherently separate these difficult boundaries in the feature space prior to classification. Additionally, exploring adaptive temperature scaling during training or extending the ordinal kernel to multi-modal patient data (e.g., combining imaging with electronic health records) presents a promising direction. Finally, validating OWMixup on multi-center clinical cohorts with high inter-rater variability will be crucial to further demonstrate its robustness.
